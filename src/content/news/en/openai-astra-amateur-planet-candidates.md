## What happened

On 24 November 2026 OpenAI published "Searching for distant planets with Astra," a post about two amateur astronomers who used Codex and Astra to search public space-telescope data for planet signals. We read the post on 27 November 2026.

OpenAI says the work produced candidates only. Confirmation, it says, could take months or years.

## Key details

All figures are from OpenAI's post. We have not checked the data or the candidates ourselves.

- **Kevin Bond:** an OpenAI engineer, who searched about 100,000 stars in NASA's Kepler data. OpenAI says he found more than 200 signals, including 10 known planets.
- **Bond's two candidates:** KIC 5689121, with a dip every 13.8 days from an object about 2.4 times Earth's diameter, and KIC 6691030, with a dip every 4.8 days from a sub-Neptune-sized object. OpenAI says an astronomer at the SETI Institute independently found both signals in the Kepler data.
- **Ethan Byrd:** founder of Primitive, who searched about 19 million stars and, per OpenAI, produced 49 first-pass candidates. He sent one candidate to the TESS team.
- **Data:** the searches used public Kepler and TESS data. The post is a research story, not a product release, and gives no pricing or availability.

## Why it matters

The result shows AI coding and reasoning tools being used by non-specialists on public datasets, which could widen who can screen astronomical data. A candidate signal is not a confirmed planet, and the post does not say how many of the amateurs' signals will survive follow-up.

One of the two people is an OpenAI employee and the post is OpenAI's own account, so independent confirmation by the TESS team or other astronomers is what to watch.
